# Saltwater intrusion and tidal flooding of bayshore farmland: literature survey (2026-10-05)

An agent ran this web survey on 2026-10-05. It follows up the screening in
`reports/bayshore_exposure.md` / FINDINGS §21: about 7 km² of cropland lies below today's MHHW, 28–40 km²
lies below MHHW + 1.0–1.5 m, and there is a tentative CDL signal that cropland is becoming wetland.

**How the references were checked.** Every reference in the list was confirmed online. Journal
articles were checked against Crossref metadata (DOI, authors, volume, pages). Reports were checked
by opening the agency PDF or page. Each source carries a tag:
**[PR]** peer-reviewed, **[AG]** government/agency report, **[NGO]** non-profit,
**[EXT]** university extension, **[CONF]** conference paper, **[NEWS]** press/magazine/press release,
**[HIST]** historical monograph. A number marked *(abstract)* was read in the abstract or a
publisher/university summary, not in the full text. A number marked *(snippet)* came only from
search-result text, because the page itself would not load. Treat those as leads. Paper titles that
could not be confirmed are listed under "Unverified leads" at the end.

## Executive summary

1. **The Delmarva evidence is strong and quantified, and New Jersey is missing from it.** Visible
   salt patches on Delmarva farmland grew from 472 ha (2011–13) to 905 ha (2016–17). In the same
   period, 8,096 ha of farmland became marsh. Losses on land within 200 m of a salt patch come to
   $39.4–107.5 M/yr (Mondal et al. 2023, Nature Sustainability) [PR]. That study covered 14 Delmarva
   counties. NJ appears on its map but was not analysed. No peer-reviewed study was found that
   quantifies cropland lost to saltwater, or cropland below the tide, in Salem or Cumberland counties.
2. **Region-wide, farmland retreats faster than forest.** From 1984 to 2022, about 100 km²
   (24,982 acres) of farmland in the Chesapeake and Delaware Bay watersheds became marsh. Marsh moves onto farmland nearly 2× as fast as onto forest, and 1.4–6.8× as often.
   Farmland moved upslope at 3.22 mm/yr, against 2.71 mm/yr for forest (Molino et al. 2026, Nature
   Sustainability) [PR]. Berms on surveyed farms slowed this to 2.14 mm/yr, against 3.45 mm/yr
   without berms *(abstract/press)*. These are the first rates for the "dikes buy time" question.
3. **The field data give a threshold close to our screening bands.** On Maryland Lower Eastern Shore
   farms, soil EC rises exponentially below **0.63 m above MHHW** (0.75 m above MSL). Sorghum kept
   relative yield above 0.5, while barley and chloride-excluding soybean often failed (Miller et al.
   2025, Discover Agriculture) [PR] *(abstract)*. The 2026 UD extension sheet puts the line at about
   2 ft above tidal level [EXT]. Our screening found 18 km² of cropland below MHHW + 0.6 m.
4. **Salt arrives by several routes.** It comes over the surface (storm tides and, more and more, ordinary
   high tides), under it (groundwater, worse in drought or when upland heads fall), and through
   ditches and tidal creeks. Ditches and drainage built to dry fields now carry salt in (Tully et al.
   2019 BioScience; Bhattachan et al. 2018; Helton et al. 2025; Kirwan et al. 2025) [PR]. SFINCS
   resolves only the surface route.
5. **The NJ bayshore has a long history of diked farmland and dike failure, mostly told in
   historical and grey literature.** In Salem County, 15,225 of 31,780 marsh acres had been drained by
   1894, and 71 meadow-bank companies had been organised by 1883. Few remain. The Greenwich bank
   (Cohansey) failed in 1989 (Sebold 1992, NPS/HABS) [HIST]. Sandy (2012) came within 1 inch of the
   Mill Creek dike crest. Repair estimates for single Cumberland levees run $0.75–14 M
   (Cumberland County Bayshore Recovery Plan 2013) [AG].
6. **The best-known NJ dike work is deliberate breaching.** PSEG's Estuary Enhancement Program
   reopened diked salt-hay farms in Commercial, Dennis and Maurice River townships, about 1,800 ha of
   former salt-hay farms *(abstract)* (Teal & Weishar 2005; Weishar et al. 2005; Hinkle & Mitsch 2005)
   [PR]. Downe Township blames more frequent flooding on that dike removal [AG]. These were hay
   impoundments, not cropland. That history matters politically for any "maintain or retreat" framing.
7. **NJ marsh and SLR assessments look at the marsh, not the farm.** Delaware Bay marsh loss is
   1.1–1.9%/decade, held low by migration into upland forest (NJDEP SAB 2020; Weis et al. 2021) [AG/PR].
   Forest has retreated 1.8 m/yr while marsh advanced 0.54 m/yr, 1930–2006, with *Phragmites* in
   between (Smith 2013, NJ shore) [PR]. SLAMM runs (statewide; EPA 2019 for 4 NJ marshes) project
   habitat, not farm exposure, and NJDEP's own panel warns of SLAMM's weaknesses. The only NJ
   farmland figure found is crude: 1,538 acres "affected by rising seas" by 2040 (AFT 2022) [NGO].
8. **Process modelling of farm salinisation is nearly all idealised or groundwater-first.** Coupled
   surface–subsurface variable-density models (HydroGeoSphere) show salinisation from storm surge and
   the protective effect of marsh buffers. Guimond & Michael (2021) used 2D idealised transects on
   Delmarva. Hu et al. (2024) drove HGS with ADCIRC on a Chinese farm and state that most studies
   "simplify spatial and temporal seawater inundation" [PR]. **No study using SFINCS or another
   compound-flood model for agricultural saltwater exposure was found.** SFINCS carries no salinity.
9. **The economics are thin.** Yield-response curves are standard: corn and many vegetables start
   losing yield at ECe 1.7 dS/m, soybean at 5.0, wheat at 6.0 (FAO 29). No hedonic study of saltwater
   intrusion and farmland *values* was found anywhere in the US. The closest is a farmland flood-risk
   discount of −13% on cropland, $3,895/acre (Wang 2021, Lancaster Co. PA) [PR]. Farmer-decision
   studies (Sudol et al. 2023; Smith et al. 2026) show a short-term resist, long-term transition
   pattern, held back by a lack of affordable options.
10. **Novelty.** A SFINCS study of Salem and Cumberland bayshore cropland would be the first
    quantitative SWI and tidal-exposure study of NJ farmland. It would also be the first dynamic
    (tide + surge + rain), field-resolved exposure product anywhere in the mid-Atlantic that includes
    farm dikes and tide gates and runs dike-failure and SLR scenarios. The literature asks for exactly
    this: the role of "construction or removal of tide gates and berms" (Mondal 2023), human
    intervention beyond "binary" protect-or-convert (Molino 2026), realistic inundation forcing for
    groundwater models (Hu 2024), and "which communities will be most impacted" (NJ STAP 2025). The
    main weakness: SFINCS sees only surface water. The value it adds over elevation relative to MHHW,
    which is already a strong predictor (Miller 2025), has to be shown, not assumed.

## 1. What is known: Delmarva, Chesapeake, North Carolina

### 1.1 Mechanisms

- **Framing.** "Saltwater intrusion is the leading edge of sea-level rise, preceding tidal
  inundation" (Tully et al. 2019, BioScience) [PR]. The paper lists five drivers: relative SLR and
  water-table position, storm frequency and tidal magnitude, drought, water extraction, and hydrologic
  connectivity (ditches, levees, canals). Very few crops tolerate sustained salinity above about 2 ppt.
  It also notes that ecosystem-service effects get less attention because the salinity changes are
  invisible.
- **Over, under, through.** Helton et al. (2025, WRR) [PR] group the pathways as overland (storm
  surge and tidal flooding), underground (groundwater), and through channels (natural and
  artificial), and show that people modify all three. They call for sensor networks that track the
  intrusion front, remote sensing that joins surface and subsurface signals, and model–data fusion.
- **Feedbacks.** Kirwan et al. (2025, Annual Review of Marine Science) [PR] argue against treating
  SWI as inundation of a static landscape. Farm management is one of the stabilising (negative)
  feedbacks. Peat collapse and subsidence are destabilising.
- **Ditches.** In eastern NC, artificial drainage both lowers land and reroutes flow, which enlarges
  the area exposed to SWI near and far from ditches. Drought and storms make this worse (Bhattachan et
  al. 2018, Elementa) [PR]. Artificial channels set the source and extent of saline intrusion in
  Albemarle–Pamlico wetlands (Manda et al. 2014, Environmental Earth Sciences) [PR]. On Maryland
  fields, EC, Cl⁻, SO₄²⁻ and P rise from field centre to ditch bank (Tully et al. 2019,
  Biogeochemistry) [PR].
- **Routine high tides, not just storms.** Mondal et al. (2023) note that SWI "increasingly results
  from frequent far-reaching seasonal high tides, as opposed to solely from infrequent powerful
  storms" [PR]. Heiss et al. (2022, WRR) [PR] used variable-density, variably saturated models to show
  that more frequent tidal flooding salinises the upper aquifer vertically. Perigean spring tides and
  the 18.6-yr nodal cycle control when this starts.
- **Storm surges and groundwater.** Surges cause simultaneous salinisation (seepage beneath flooded
  ground) and freshening (Paldor & Michael 2021, WRR) [PR]. Upland groundwater declines push shallow
  saline water inland at Delmarva marsh-to-upland transects (Pratt et al. 2025, GRL) [PR]. A
  2021–2024 groundwater and soil-moisture dataset for 3 agricultural and 3 forested Delmarva sites
  records soil salinisation during surges and groundwater salinisation during droughts (Pratt et al.
  2025, Data in Brief) [PR].
- **Recovery.** After a surge, soil salinity rises or falls depending on the ratio of
  evapotranspiration to rainfall and on soil type (Nordio & Fagherazzi 2024, WRR) [PR]. This matters
  for any "duration of exposure" metric.

### 1.3 Soil and crop response in the field

- Soil EC rises exponentially below 0.75 m above MSL and **0.63 m above MHHW**. Over a 4-yr rotation,
  sorghum held relative yield above 0.5, barley failed in saturated winter soils, and CE soybean often
  gave zero yield. Available P fell by up to 50% in SWI plots (Miller, Ramalho de Barros, Schulenburg &
  Tully 2025) [PR] *(abstract)*.
- UD extension (Miller et al., Feb 2026): ground less than 2 ft above tidal levels showed "sharp
  increases" in soil salinity. Tolerance ranks barley > sorghum > soybean. Perennials (switchgrass,
  *Spartina patens*) or easements are suggested for the worst ground [EXT].
- Candidate restoration species on salt-affected fields were tested by de Barros et al. (2025, AGEE)
  [PR] (title and metadata verified only). Novel plant assemblages of weeds, natives and invasives form
  on salinised fields (Gedan & Fernández-Pascual 2019, J. Veg. Sci.) [PR]. Weissman & Tully (2020,
  Ecosphere) [PR] report nutrient release to porewater and surface water from SWI-affected soils.

### 1.4 Farmers and structures

- Maryland and Virginia producers mostly plan to **resist or accept** for 5 years and **transition**
  later. They lack "effective and/or affordable management options to resist" (Sudol et al. 2023, J.
  Environ. Manage.) [PR]. Virginia Eastern Shore farmers prefer keeping farmland to marsh migration
  (Smith et al. 2026, Rural Sociology; 12 interviews) [PR].
- Waterfowl impoundments on salt-affected farmland: 1,684 found by deep learning on 1 m topobathy
  (6.6 km²). 60% sit in the 1-m-SLR migration pathway, and building rose after CREP began in 1997.
  Federal and state incentives may be building barriers to migration (Molino & Kirwan 2025, J.
  Environ. Manage.) [PR].
- Molino et al. 2026 field surveys: berms slow loss, many berms are abandoned, and "new construction
  is limited because of complicated permitting" (press) [PR/NEWS].

### 1.5 North Carolina

Bhattachan et al. (2018) [PR] map vulnerability on the Albemarle–Pamlico peninsula, where about 24%
of land is agricultural, according to Guimond & Michael (2021). Bhattachan et al. (2019, Natural
Hazards) [PR] link residents' risk perception to physical exposure. USDA's Southeast Climate Hub
synthesis (Gibson et al. 2021, GTR SRS-259) [AG] gives salinity yield-decline curves by commodity and
says landowners lack resources to manage salinisation. A co-author is the NRCS Cape May Plant
Materials Center manager.

## 2. The NJ side of Delaware Bay (and Delaware for comparison)

### 2.1 Banked meadows and salt-hay farms: history

From Sebold (1992), *From Marsh to Farm* (NPS/HABS) [HIST]:
- Salem County: **15,225 of 31,780 marsh acres drained by 1894**, mainly along Salem Creek. Much of
  Cumberland's Maurice and Cohansey frontage was also embanked.
- **71 meadow companies** had been organised in Salem County by 1883: Mannington 18, Lower Penns Neck
  17, Lower Alloways Creek 14, Elsinboro 8, Upper Penns Neck 8, Salem City 5, Upper Alloways Creek 1.
  The meadow-company law dates from 1788 and was amended through 1957.
- Banked land was worth $100–300/acre against $1–5 unbanked. Banking cost $1–3 per rod.
  Maintenance was $0.50–$2/acre/yr. Crops included hay, corn, wheat, potatoes, tomatoes and strawberries.
- Decline came between 1880 and 1910 and accelerated through WWII. Causes were storms, maintenance
  costs, Depression, labour lost to the war, and muskrat pelts paying better than crops. Active
  companies cited: **West Branch of Stow Creek, Silver Lake, Town Bank** (Salem) and the **Greenwich
  Bank Company** (Cumberland, founded 1806). The Greenwich bank **failed in 1989** and was repaired with
  state and local funds. (Silver Lake is one of the leveed systems in our screening.)
- Supawna Meadows NWR (Salem): storms eventually breached all refuge dikes, and farmers' last diking
  effort ended in the late 1930s (USFWS refuge plan) [AG] *(snippet)*.

### 2.2 PSEG Estuary Enhancement Program (restoration by breaching)

- PSEG's Salem/Hope Creek permit mitigation restored former salt-hay impoundments in **Commercial,
  Dennis and Maurice River townships** by breaching dikes and dredging channels 300–4,000 m into the
  marsh. The marsh plain had subsided after more than 50 years of diking (Teal & Peterson 2005; Teal &
  Weishar 2005; Weishar, Teal & Hinkle 2005; Hinkle & Mitsch 2005, all Ecol. Eng. 25) [PR]. Program
  scale: about 4,000 ha of marsh, of which about 1,800 ha were former diked salt-hay farms *(abstract)*.
  Site sizes of Dennis 227 ha and Commercial 1,504 ha appear in indexed abstracts *(snippet)*. The
  design used hydrodynamic and sediment models. Alloway Creek (Salem) is also an EEP watershed site.
  PSEG describes the program as more than 20,000 acres restored, enhanced or preserved *(snippet)*.
- Downe Township's cross-acceptance report says flooding "is more prevalent since NJDEP and PSEG
  removed the dike system", and roads now flood at high tide and full moon [AG].
- Fortescue: a 322-acre impounded salt-hay farm was breached by a 1972 storm (USFWS document on
  regulations.gov) [AG] *(snippet)*.

### 2.3 Dike condition and failures on working farmland

- **Greenwich Twp (Cumberland)**, NJDEP Coastal Community Vulnerability & Resilience Assessment Pilot
  (2011) [AG]: Greenwich is already seeing "saltwater intrusion into freshwater resources" and the
  shift from freshwater to salt marsh. A former agricultural dike at Greenwich Pier failed in the
  preceding decade, and tide now flows past the Bacons Neck Road weir into a formerly freshwater
  wetland. Diked areas on the Cohansey flood in spring tides. Modelled Category-1 depths are 5.3 ft
  at Greenwich Road near the dike and 3.5 ft at Bacons Neck Road near the dike. The report also warns
  that "inward movement of tidal waters could easily topple existing agricultural dikes and
  contaminate freshwater resources and wells".
- **Cumberland County Delaware Bayshore Recovery Plan (Dec 2013)** [AG]: Greenwich dikes are "earthen,
  with a clay core topped by sand and gravel" and "get carved out in storms". **Sandy came within one
  inch of the top of the Mill Creek dike** on the Cohansey. The Matt's Landing dike made a 130-yard
  breach (Sandy, then Dec 2012) and cost **$750,000** to repair. Repair estimates: Maple Ave levee
  $3.5 M; Matt's Landing impoundment levee $2.5 M; Pine Mount Creek levee $2.4–14 M; Mill Creek /
  Molly Wheaton Run / Mounces Creek levees $1.5–3 M; Market Lane levee $0.75 M. Levees on navigable
  rivers need USACE permits. The plan cites a *South Jersey Levee Inventory* (NRCS / NJDEP Bureau of
  Dam Safety, 2010), which was not located.
- **Sheppard Farms, Cedarville**: asparagus, sweet corn, peppers and cucumbers about 2 mi from the
  bay behind dikes and tide gates. The dikes were raised 2 ft after a Sandy breach (Civil Eats 2024)
  [NEWS].
- **Salem**: Mannington Meadows is a 6,000-acre tidal system ringed by farmland, once diked and farmed
  until storms breached the levees (NJ Conservation Foundation) [NGO] *(snippet)*. No source was found
  that documents Middle Neck, the largest exposed system in our screening.

### 2.4 Adaptation, easements and buyouts in NJ

- **Cohansey Meadows (Fairfield Twp)**: corn, bean and hay rotation abandoned to SWI. The farm now grows
  *Spartina patens* salt hay under an NRCS Conservation Innovation Grant ("Spartina patens Saltwater
  Intrusion Initiative") and is exploring Wetland Reserve Easements, EQIP and CSP (NJ Audubon 2025
  [NGO]; Civil Eats 2024 [NEWS]). NRCS's Cape May Plant Materials Center ran a related study (2024
  report, not read).
- **NRCS**: in FY2023 NJ closed two Wetland Reserve Easements, one in Salem County. An RCPP
  "Delaware Bay Soil and Water Quality Protection Initiative" (NJ Conservation Foundation) targets
  Salem and parts of Cumberland and Gloucester [AG] *(snippet)*.
- **Blue Acres**: residential buyouts in Downe Twp (Gandys Beach, Money Island, Bay Point,
  Fortescue) after Sandy [AG/NEWS]. No farmland buyout program specific to SWI was found in NJ.

### 2.5 Marsh status, migration, and SLR projections

- NJDEP Science Advisory Board (2020) [AG], published as Weis et al. 2021, Anthropocene Coasts [PR]:
  Delaware Bay marsh loss is 1.1–1.9%/decade, offset by migration into low maritime forest. 12 Delaware
  Bay SETs show +1.16 to +6.89 mm/yr. SLAMM runs statewide (NJ Flood Mapper), but the board warns that
  "predicted changes may or may not materialize" and says policy should rest on observed change. It
  also notes Greenwich's exposure to saltwater intrusion.
- Smith (2013, PLoS ONE) [PR], NJ Delaware Estuary 1930–2006 across 101 km of ecotone: forest retreat
  141 m (1.8 m/yr) against marsh migration 42 m (0.54 m/yr). *Phragmites* fills the gap.
- EPA (2019) SLAMM for 7 Lower Delaware Bay marshes, 4 in NJ (Dividing, Lower Maurice, Dennis, Reeds
  Beach) [AG]. CRSSA (Lathrop & Love 2007) mapped vulnerability of NJ coastal habitats [AG/univ.].
  Both target habitat, not cropland.
- Ghost forests: the Atlantic-coast map (Yeung et al. 2025) [PR] was reported in NJ news for Cape May
  and the Mullica, not Salem or Cumberland [NEWS].
- AFT *Farms Under Threat 2040* (2022): 1,538 acres of NJ farmland "affected by rising seas" by 2040
  under mid-range SLR (elevation only) [NGO].
- **NJ STAP 2025** (Rutgers/NJDEP) [AG]: likely NJ SLR of 0.9–1.7 ft by 2050. By 2100, 2.2–3.8 ft
  under intermediate emissions, up to 4.5 ft with rapid ice loss. Atlantic City coastal flood days went
  from fewer than 1/yr (1950s) to 12/yr (2007–24) and could reach 29–148/yr by 2050. The summary says
  "further studies are needed to identify what communities will be most impacted by saltwater
  intrusion". *For our screening bands, MHHW + 0.3 m is about 2050 likely-low, + 0.6 m about 2050
  likely-high, and + 1.0 m about the 2100 intermediate central range (the STAP figures are for
  Atlantic City).*

### 2.6 Water-supply salinity in NJ tributaries

- USGS (Closson, Suro & Niemoczynski 2026, SIR 2025-5090) [AG] estimated the daily salt-front location
  (1,100 µS/cm) for Jul 2021–Jul 2024. It ranged from RM 10.8 to 23.2 on the Maurice and from RM 18.5
  to 20.4 on the Cohansey. The report cites USDA and Cumberland farmers' concern for irrigation and
  crops.
- NJDEP 2020 Scientific Report on Climate Change [AG]: with 1 m SLR, the Delaware River salt front may
  move 5.5–8.6 mi upriver. NJGWS/USGS began a Kirkwood-Cohansey monitoring network along Delaware Bay
  *(snippet, via Rutgers NJCRC summary)*.

### 2.7 Delaware (comparison)

- DNREC *Preparing for Tomorrow's High Tide* (2012) [AG]: 8–11% of the state's land (with wetlands) is
  inundated at 0.5–1.5 m. 2–4% of highly productive soils are exposed. 81% of impounded wetlands are
  inundated at 0.5 m, up to 99% at 1.0–1.5 m. 39–78% of the state's 50 miles of dams, dikes and levees
  could be inundated by 2100; Kent County's dikes mainly protect wildlife areas.
- Delaware's three counties are inside Mondal et al. (2023): area within 200 m of salt patches up 68%.
  Molino et al. (2026) cover the Delaware Bay watershed but give no NJ/DE breakdown in what could be
  read.

**What was not found for NJ:** any peer-reviewed estimate of NJ cropland below tidal datums, lost to
SWI, or salt-patched; any per-field exposure modelling; any study of the condition or performance of
private farm dikes and tide gates; any farmland-value study.

## 3. Modelling approaches

| Class | Examples | Gives | Limits for this question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Remote sensing / empirical | Mondal 2023 (1 m imagery, salt patches); Sarupria 2025 (Sentinel-2 unmixing); Molino 2026 (Landsat 1984–2022); Gedan 2020; our CDL screen | Observed extent, trends, hotspots | No mechanism; salt patches are transient; can't do scenarios such as a dike breach |
| Elevation / landscape (bathtub, SLAMM, GIS indices) | SLAMM (EPA 2019; NJ statewide); Molino 2022 (1 m migration); Bhattachan 2018 (drainage-aware vulnerability); Osland 2022; AFT 2022; UMD dashboard | Long-term habitat change, exposure ranking | Static; ignores gates, duration and rain; NJDEP SAB flags SLAMM's weaknesses |
| Coupled surface–subsurface, variable density | **HydroGeoSphere**: Guimond & Michael 2021 (2D, Delmarva-like transects); Yang et al. 2013 (North Sea, tides + surge); Paldor & Michael 2021; **ADCIRC → HGS** Hu et al. 2024 (coastal farm, China) | Salt in soil and aquifer, recovery time, yield (Guimond) | Mostly 2D or idealised; costly; surface forcing simplified (Hu 2024's stated gap) |
| Groundwater-only (SEAWAT/SUTRA/MODFLOW-type) | Heiss et al. 2022 (variable-density, variably saturated, 80-yr tidal flooding runs) | Aquifer salinity under SLR and tides | No overland routing or dikes |
| Vadose / soil salinity (HYDRUS, etc.) | Mostly reclaimed coastal land in China; Nordio & Fagherazzi 2024 (post-storm ET/rain) | Root-zone EC through time | Needs a flooding/ponding upper boundary |
| 2D hydrodynamic | SFINCS (Leijnse et al. 2021); Delft3D-FM (dyke-breach realignment); HEC-RAS (tidal paddy farmland, Indonesia); hydrodynamic + sediment modelling for the PSEG breaches (Weishar 2005) | Inundation extent, depth, duration, frequency | No salt; no groundwater |

- **SFINCS in or near our domain:** Maduwantha et al. (2026, HESS) [PR] ran 5,000 synthetic compound
  events with SFINCS for Gloucester City NJ, just north of Big Timber Creek. They found that mean sea
  level and tidal phase alone change flood depth by more than 1.0–1.2 m. This supports careful
  boundary timing.
- **SFINCS capability:** the documentation lists no salinity or tracer transport; for salinity it
  points to Delft3D FM. It includes curve-number and other infiltration, weirs and levees, and simple
  drainage structures. Deltares lists, without a citation, an application to "tropical cyclone and
  sea level rise driven flooding in polders of Bangladesh", which is the nearest analogue to diked
  farmland.
- **Answer to Q3:** no published SFINCS (or other compound-flood model) study of *agricultural
  saltwater exposure* was found. The closest analogues are Guimond & Michael 2021 (HGS, idealised,
  with yield and $) and Hu et al. 2024 (ADCIRC + HGS on one farm). A practical design here: SFINCS
  gives per-cell flood frequency, depth and duration of *tidal-origin* water. Tidal origin can come
  from paired runs with and without tide, or from cells wetted only when tide exceeds the gate or
  dike. Those time series then drive a 1D soil-salinity model (HYDRUS-type) or a 2D HGS transect at a
  few fields, with Miller et al. (2025)'s elevation-above-MHHW threshold as an empirical check.

From FAO Irrigation & Drainage Paper 29 Rev. 1 (Ayers & Westcot 1985), Table 4, after Maas & Hoffman
1977 [AG/PR]:

The bayshore vegetable crops (peppers, sweet corn, tomatoes, cucumbers, asparagus) are mostly in the
sensitive group, so a given salt exposure costs more here than in Delmarva corn and soybean. These
are steady-state root-zone curves. Waterlogging and episodic exposure are not covered, and field
evidence suggests the curves need local calibration (Gibson et al. 2021 [AG]; Grieve, Grattan & Maas
2012 [PR chapter]).

### 4.2 Loss estimates

- Delmarva: $39.4 M/yr (all soy) to $107.5 M/yr (all corn) for farmland within 200 m of salt patches,
  assuming 100% profit loss, 2016–17 (Mondal et al. 2023) [PR]. Press materials also cite $70.7 M for
  a corn/soy mix.
- Guimond & Michael (2021) [PR]: with surges of 3.0 m or more and 0.1% slope, 200–400 m of marsh
  migration onto fields protects yield landward and "may benefit farmers financially". Below a 3 m
  surge, or above 400 m of migration, yields are not maintained. They call for "more robust
  compensation programs" for landowners who let wetland develop.

### 4.3 Land values

- **No hedonic study of SWI or SLR on farmland values was found in the US**, after several targeted
  searches. This is a clear gap for the ag-econ collaborators.
- Nearest analogues: Wang (2021, Risk Analysis) [PR] finds cropland parcels in the 1% annual-chance
  floodplain sell for **13% less, $3,895/acre in 2015$** (95% CI $510–7,352), using a boundary
  discontinuity in Lancaster Co. PA; noncrop farmland shows no effect. Li, Nadolnyak & Hartarska (2019,
  Land Use Policy) [PR] find that flood risk *slows* farmland-to-urban conversion (Lee Co. FL).
- Historical: banked meadow traded at $100–300/acre against $1–5 for unbanked marsh in 19th-century
  NJ (Sebold 1992) [HIST]. That is the original "value of the dike".

### 4.4 Adaptation options

- Salt-tolerant crops (sorghum, barley), perennial grasses, and salt hay (*S. patens*) as a crop and
  restoration plug source: Miller et al. 2025 [PR], UD extension 2026 [EXT], Cohansey Meadows
  [NGO/NEWS], Gibson et al. 2021 [AG].
- Easements and set-asides: ACEP-WRE, CREP (NWF 2024 [NEWS]; Molino & Kirwan 2025 note that CREP may
  encourage impoundments that block migration [PR]). Maryland has a state SWI adaptation plan (2019,
  updated 2024) [AG], and UMD is building the OARS decision tool [EXT]. NJ has no equivalent plan.
- Resist, Accept, Direct framing of farmer choices (Sudol et al. 2023) [PR].
- Comparative: Velilla et al. 2025, "Too salty to farm" (Restoration Ecology) [PR], on rethinking
  coastal land use (Netherlands).

### 4.5 Dike and tide-gate costs

- 19th-century NJ: banking $1–3/rod; maintenance $0.50–2/acre/yr (Sebold 1992) [HIST].
- Recent Cumberland County: single levee repairs $0.75–14 M; Matt's Landing breach repair $0.75 M
  (Bayshore Recovery Plan 2013) [AG].
- No study was found that sets dike or tide-gate maintenance cost against the value of the farmland
  protected on the Delaware Bay, the "maintain vs retreat" calculation.

## 5. Novelty assessment

### Gaps the literature calls out

| Gap (quoted or paraphrased) | Source |
|---|---|
| "Greater understanding of how human activities exacerbate or reduce salt patches and marsh, such as the construction or removal of tide gates and berms, should be of particular interest to policy-makers"; process models needed for ditches, reclamation legacies, water tables | Mondal et al. 2023 |
| Land-use response is usually modelled as binary (instant conversion or indefinite protection); how human intervention changes climate-driven conversion is unclear, especially in rural areas | Molino et al. 2026 |

| Producers lack effective and affordable options; need flexible resist-then-direct support | Sudol et al. 2023 |

| Flood risk to farmland is understudied compared with urban flood risk | Wang 2021 |

### What would be new in a SFINCS study of Salem and Cumberland bayshore cropland

1. **Geography.** No quantitative study of SWI or tidal exposure of NJ Delaware Bay farmland exists in
   the peer-reviewed record. Mondal (2023) excludes NJ, and Molino (2026) reports Chesapeake and
   Delaware Bay together. Our first numbers (7 km² below MHHW, 18 km² below MHHW + 0.6 m, 40 km² below
   MHHW + 1.5 m; 88% of the below-MHHW area behind NLD levees) are already new.
2. **Dike and tide-gate dependence, made explicit.** No published model represents farm dikes as
   weirs and tide gates as one-way drains at field scale (the repo already does: 25 check valves for
   18 farm-dike systems, `reports/dike_outlets.md`). Running breach, gate-failure and crest-raise
   scenarios answers Mondal's and Molino's open question directly. It gives the ag-econ side a
   protected-acreage-per-dike-km metric to set against the levee cost figures above.
3. **Dynamic exposure metrics, not bathtub.** Field-level frequency, depth and **duration** of
   tidal-origin flooding, and the compound case (rain trapped behind gates that cannot drain as low
   tide rises) are not available anywhere for mid-Atlantic farmland. The drainage failure mode is
   barely discussed in the literature. Our dike-outlet analysis already finds negative drain margins
   at Middle Neck and others, so gates may fail to drain before dikes overtop.
4. **Link to observed loss data.** No study joins modelled saltwater exposure to crop-insurance
   indemnities. The county × month loss panel makes that possible, though SWI losses may be booked
   under other causes. CDL wetland conversion plus Sentinel-2 salt-patch unmixing (Sarupria 2025,
   transferable by design) provide independent validation.
5. **Vegetable systems.** NJ bayshore crops include salt-sensitive vegetables (ECe thresholds
   1.5–2.5 dS/m) as well as corn and soybean, so damage per exposure-day is higher than in the
   corn/soy-based Delmarva estimates.

### Honest limits (what reviewers will ask)

- **SFINCS does not carry salt or groundwater.** Exposure has to be defined as tidal-origin surface
  water. The subsurface route (drought, falling heads, ditch seepage), which Delmarva studies stress,
  needs a coupled or offline groundwater/soil model, at least at a few transects.
- **Elevation relative to MHHW already predicts a lot** (Miller 2025; our screening). The paper has to
  show what SFINCS adds: gates, connectivity, creek-head tidal amplification, duration, and rain
  ponding.
- **Validation data are thin in NJ**: no salt-patch inventory and no soil-EC survey. Field EC
  transects or a Sentinel-2 salt-patch map of Salem and Cumberland would be a high-value addition.
- The **CDL wetland-conversion signal** needs an imagery check before it is used as evidence.
