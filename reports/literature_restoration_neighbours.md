# Restoring tide behind a shared dike: what happens to the neighbours? Literature check (2026-10-06)

An agent ran this web check on 2026-10-06 to test FINDINGS §30 before it is shared. §30 says
that re-opening the outlet of the Middle Neck polder (Salem County; 1,135 ha leveed; 98 farm
fields; holds the 7.5 ha WRE 330636) floods ~470 ha of other farm fields behind the same bank,
while water levels outside the bank change by < 1 mm (≤ 0.1 cm in mean daily high water within 3 km).
It adds to `reports/literature_saltwater.md` (§2.2–2.3) and does not repeat it.

**How sources were checked.** Each source carries a type tag: **[PR]** peer-reviewed, **[AG]**
government/agency, **[NGO]** non-profit or professional body, **[NEWS]**. It also carries the depth
it was read to: **[full]** = the full text was opened (or the relevant pages of it); **[abs]** = only
the abstract (publisher or OpenAlex); **[snip]** = only search-result text or a secondary summary.
Treat [snip] items as leads. Quotes are copied from the text that was opened.

---

## 1. Do restoration schemes take the whole embanked unit, or build a new line?

**Short answer: a landward line of defence is the norm. A scheme either runs to high ground or
puts a new or existing bank between the tide and the land that stays dry. No case was found where a
scheme flooded unacquired land behind the same bank without protection or an agreement.**

- **Europe-wide census.** van den Hoven et al. (2022) [PR][full] sorted 90 European coastal
  realignments by their landward dike:
  - 36 have a newly built landward dike;
  - 39 reuse an existing "hinterland" dike of a multi-dike system;
  - 15 are realignments inside summer polders, behind an existing winter dike.

  Every case in the census has a landward realigned dike. The schemes are small: mean 155 ha,
  median 37.5 ha, range 0.8–3,600 ha.
  - At **Hedwige-Prosperpolder** (465 ha, Scheldt), "one part of the realigned dike was newly built
    to separate the Prosperpolder into North and South". That is a cross-dike through one
    embanked polder, the same move as our set-back scenario.
  - **Lillo Potpolder** (10 ha) "was realigned in two compartments".
- **Review statement.** Schuerch et al. (2022) [PR][full]: "Most newly established defences on
  the landward side of MR schemes are at least as high and strong as the original defences to
  reassure local communities."
- **UK cases, from the Defra/EA *Managed Realignment Review* (Halcrow et al. 2002) [AG][full]:**
  - **Tollesbury** (21 ha, 1995): "Despite the higher ground boundary, it was necessary to provide
    a secondary defence for the site to prevent any inundation of adjacent agricultural land."
  - **Orplands** (45 ha, 1994): "no secondary defences were necessary", because the site rises
    onto London Clay high ground.
  - **Abbotts Hall** (~115 ha planned): five breaches. "The remaining lengths of seawall will be
    left in place". The site was chosen so that high ground lies between the breaches.
  - **Brancaster**: a clay embankment 300 m landward protects the rest of the SPA. The flooded
    grazing marsh was "purchased with DEFRA grant aid". The golf club built "another new
    counterwall" to protect its practice ground.
  - **Thorngumbald / Paull Holme Strays** (Humber): new embankment set back ~500 m and ~200 m. A
    pumping station was built "to convey flow from Thorngumbald Drain over the new embankment".
    So the neighbours' land drainage was re-plumbed.
  - **Humber policy**: the Humber "has very large flood cells, so that where realignment is
    adopted a set-back line of defence will generally be required". The EA expected "to buy the
    land needed". For owners who keep their land, it named "financial compensation for change of
    use or purchase of easements to flood" as alternatives.
- **Other UK schemes:**
  - **Freiston Shore** (2002): the inland bank was strengthened and the seaward bank breached.
    The land was bought by the RSPB (EA/Defra FD1911 summary) [AG][full].
  - **Medmerry** (2013): "a new 7km flood embankment, parts of which are almost 2km inland", with
    ~183 ha intertidal (Landscape Institute case study) [NGO][full].
  - **Steart** (2014): "New embankments were built a kilometre or more inland", and 300 ha of
    farmland was flooded (WWT) [NGO][full].
- **US Pacific NW.** At **Fir Island Farm** (Skagit, WA; 131 acres, 2016) WDFW built a 5,800 ft
  set-back dike, removed 3,400 ft of sea dike, and added "a 7-acre drainage storage pond and pump
  station" and relocated tide gates. The aim was to "reduce flooding, maintain drainage, and
  prevent saltwater intrusion on surrounding farmland" [AG][full].
- **US Northeast.** The **Herring River** (Cape Cod; ~1,100 acres behind a 1909 dike) is restoring
  tide to a floodplain shared with private land [AG][full].
  - Roads are being raised 3–4 ft, and "protection of low-lying property within the restoration
    project area" includes moving wells and raising driveways (NPS). The project FAQ adds "a small
    berm … adjacent to one dwelling" [NGO][full].
  - A Mill Creek tide-control structure is planned to "prevent the flooding of upstream private
    properties" (USGS SIR 2019-5145) [AG][abs].
  - Concerns about flooding of private property had earlier stopped the town from opening the old
    tide gate further (search summary of NPS DEIS) [snip].

## 2. Documented effects on neighbouring land

**Short answer: projects treat effects on neighbours as real. They put money into drainage,
seepage and salt protection, and those protections are documented. Measured cases of harm on
neighbouring farmland are few, and most of them come from leaky or failed structures, not
designed restorations. For the Delaware Bay, the only claim found (Downe Township) is unsupported.**

- **Groundwater salt on adjacent land.** At Kentuck Slough (Coos Bay, OR), leaky tide gates let
  salt water into the slough. This caused "an increase in the amount of salt water that intrudes
  into adjacent land via groundwater flow", which "negatively affected the quality of the soil
  during the summer months". The landowners said "any increase would not be acceptable". The
  replacement gates were designed so as not to increase the saltwater influx (Giannico & Souder
  2018, OSU/OWEB tide-gate review, App. B) [AG][full].
- **Speed of salinisation inside a de-embanked site.** In a Dutch de-embanked summer polder the
  topsoil salinised quickly. Groundwater at 1.2 m depth "increased slowly throughout the full
  4-year period" and still did not reach seawater (Veenklaas et al. 2015) [PR][abs]. Read across
  to neighbours: a salt effect through groundwater would lag years behind the surface flooding.
- **Off-site morphology.** At Freiston Shore, drainage from the new site caused sheet flow on the
  adjacent intertidal flat. Creeks there extended headward "up to 400 m yr⁻¹, over 20 times
  greater than … natural conditions". They silted up again after ~3 years (Symonds & Collins 2007)
  [PR][abs].
- **Landowner conflict.**
  - Schuerch et al. (2022) [PR][full]: "Public opposition is considered a key obstacle to MR
    implementation … resulting in the delay or abandonment of schemes".
  - The Kilchis Preserve (Tillamook, OR) "sparked controversy among the agricultural community and
    neighboring landowners". The second-highest flood on record (Dec 2015) led "to fears that the
    project was responsible" (Giannico & Souder 2018, App. B) [AG][full].
  - Skagit farmers tie their support to replacing drainage: "When infrastructure is removed, like
    the dike and tide gate in this project, those have to be replaced" (same source, quoting
    Capital Press) [AG][full].
- **Delaware Bay: PSEG Estuary Enhancement Program.**
  - What is confirmed: three diked salt-hay farms in **Commercial, Dennis and Maurice River
    townships**, 1,780 ha in total, were reopened in 1996–98 by dike breaching and channel
    excavation (Hinkle & Mitsch 2005) [PR][snip]. The settlement set up advisory committees after
    "extensive negotiations with stakeholders" (Weinstein et al. 2001) [PR][snip].
  - What was not found: **no primary document saying whether PSEG built protection for adjacent
    uplands, or whether neighbours flooded.** The Ecological Engineering vol. 25 papers (Teal &
    Peterson; Balletto et al.; Philipp; Weishar et al.; Hinkle & Mitsch; Teal & Weishar) are
    paywalled, and their abstracts are withheld from OpenAlex and Semantic Scholar. PSEG's
    Commercial Township site sheet (2007) [AG][full] is directions only.
  - **Not verified.**
- **The Downe Township claim.** The source is the Cross Acceptance Response Template for Downe
  (Cumberland County Planning, approved 4 June 2025) [AG][full].
  - What it says: "The Township notes that flooding is more prevalent since NJDEP and PSEG removed
    the dike system that historically protected its communities", and it asks the State "to
    recreate the dike system". It also blames "routine flooding, as result of high tide-full moon
    and insufficient flood protection (ie. removal of dike system)" for the need to raise roads.
  - What it lacks: it names **no dike, site, date, or data**.
  - PSEG's salt-hay restorations are in the neighbouring townships, not in Downe. Which dikes
    Downe means is **unknown**. Treat the statement as a municipal assertion, not evidence.
- **Delaware: Prime Hook NWR.** This was accidental dune breaching into 1980s freshwater
  impoundments, not a planned restoration. Unit II "is transitioning to open water, and has not
  buffered the uplands well" (USFWS fact sheet) [AG][full]. Residents' only road flooded repeatedly
  (Cape Gazette) [NEWS][snip], and neighbouring farmers reported salt intrusion (Bay to Bay News
  commentary) [NEWS][snip].
- **Local NJ analogue (already in `literature_saltwater.md` §2.3).** A failed farm dike at
  Greenwich Pier let the tide past the Bacons Neck Road weir into a formerly freshwater wetland
  (NJDEP 2011).

## 3. Far-field water levels: does adding a polder's storage change levels outside it?

**Short answer: it can, but only when the added area is large relative to the estuary, or sits in a
confined reach, or when the event is a surge that the site can store. The sign can go either way.
For a ~1–11 km² site on a large estuary, millimetre-scale daily-tide effects are plausible, but
cm-scale effects in a confined tidal creek or during surges cannot be ruled out from the literature.**

- **Scheldt model.** Attenuation of high water along the estuary grows with wetland area "up to a
  threshold wetland size". A wetland placed further upstream has a larger effect on upstream high
  water (Smolders et al. 2015) [PR][abs]. Schuerch et al. (2022) [PR][full], citing Smolders:
  losing ~3,000 ha of marsh could raise storm water levels in the wider estuary by up to 19 cm.
- **Large sites can raise levels.** "Large realignment sites near the mouth of an estuary have the
  potential to raise water levels throughout the estuary". Sites that fill slowly through friction
  have less effect (Pontee 2015) [PR][abs]. Schuerch et al. (2022): schemes "in the wrong part of
  the estuary may lead to increases in estuarine water levels".
- **Small sites can amplify surges.** A model of Freiston Shore: "surges are amplified for
  all but the largest two site scenarios". Real attenuation (up to 18 cm km⁻¹) needed widths
  > 1,148 m (Kiesel et al. 2021) [PR][abs].
- **Delaware Bay itself.** Letting low-lying land flood under sea-level rise *lowers* tidal range,
  while walling it off raises range up-estuary. The ratio of range change to SLR spans −0.2 to
  +0.25 in Delaware Bay (Lee, Li & Zhang 2017) [PR][abs]. That result is for estuary-wide land
  conversion, not one polder.
- **Single-site case.** Alkborough Flats (440 ha, at the head of the Humber, overflow-weir design)
  is said to lower 1-in-200-year levels in the upper estuary by "over 150mm" (ICE / Wheeler et al.
  2008) [AG/CONF][snip]. This is an extreme-event storage effect in a confined reach, not a
  daily-tide effect.
- **Several polders in sequence.** In a Western Scheldt Delft3D study, opening "transitional
  polders" in sequence lowered tidal range. The reduction was "negligible compared to the increase
  in tidal range due to historic dredging" (Weisscher et al. 2022) [PR][abs].

## 4. Subsidence of long-diked land, and open water before marsh

**Short answer: well supported. Long-diked marsh is usually tens of centimetres to about a metre
lower than natural marsh. When the tide returns it tends to become open water or mudflat first, and
on Delaware Bay many such sites have stayed that way.**

- **Magnitudes:**
  - **Herring River**: the diked floodplain is "about 70 cm (over two feet) lower than the natural
    marsh surface just downstream" (NPS) [AG][full].
  - **Salmon River, Oregon**: diked site 0.30–0.40 m below control marsh. Another site was still
    34.9 cm below control 11 years after breaching (Giannico & Souder 2018 summaries of Mitchell
    1981 and Frenkel & Morlan) [AG][full; secondary].
  - **Crescent Harbor, WA**: ~3 ft (same source) [AG][full; secondary].
  - **Sacramento–San Joaquin Delta** (extreme case): former marsh is more than 5 m below mean tide
    level (J. Coastal Res. 2020 paper on Delta tidal marsh sedimentation) [PR][snip].
- **Mechanism, and a further drop on re-flooding.** Seawater added to seasonally flooded diked peat
  caused "6–8 cm of sediment subsidence" and a 10-fold sulfide increase in microcosms (Portnoy &
  Giblin 1997) [PR][abs].
- **Delaware Bay, NJ:**
  - Smith, Hafner & Niles (2017) [PR][snip, journal highlights]: "More than half of Delaware
    Estuary, NJ … marshes were historically impounded". Most marsh-to-water conversion over 85
    years was in former impoundments. Never-impounded marsh is higher and has more high marsh.
  - The American Littoral Society's Thompsons Beach project description (USFWS/NPS planning
    record) [NGO][full] adds:
    - "approximately 21.6% of formerly impounded marsh in Delaware Bay has not revegetated, has
      become open water or mud flat after impoundments were breached or dikes removed", against
      "only 0.5%" of never-impounded marsh since 1930;
    - before PSEG's work, "much of the formerly vegetated salt hay farm was open water and
      mudflats";
    - PSEG dredged "more than seven miles of new tidal channels", but the elevation deficits "have
      not been recovered across some of the area".
- **European de-embankments.**
  - Tollesbury's low northern half was below salt-marsh level and had "not yet colonised after 7
    years" (Halcrow et al. 2002) [AG][full].
  - Realigned UK sites stay dominated by pioneer species compared with reference marshes (Mossman
    et al. 2012) [PR][abs].

## Bottom line for our Middle Neck result

- **Supported: opening a shared bank floods everything behind it, and that is exactly why
  practice avoids doing it.**
  - All 90 European schemes in the census have a landward dike. Tollesbury got a secondary bank
    "to prevent any inundation of adjacent agricultural land". Hedwige-Prosper got a new
    cross-dike splitting one polder. The Humber's large flood cells "generally" need a set-back
    line.
  - The ~470 ha is therefore best presented as the cost of a design nobody would build. The
    **set-back run (13.6 km inner dike) is the realistic comparison.**
  - For scale, 13.6 km is about twice Medmerry's 7 km new bank, so its cost should be flagged.
- **Supported: real projects pay to protect the neighbours' drainage, not only their flooding.**
  Examples are pumping stations (Thorngumbald, Fir Island), a drainage storage pond (Fir Island),
  a tide-control structure for one sub-basin (Herring River, Mill Creek) and raised roads and
  wells (Herring River). A set-back at Middle Neck needs its own outlet and gate for the 98 fields,
  which our setback scenario includes. Its operating cost belongs in the economics.
- **Qualified: "< 1 mm outside the bank".**
  - This is consistent with the literature for daily tides on a large estuary. Estuary-scale
    effects come from thousands of hectares (Scheldt) or confined, upstream sites.
  - But Alkborough (440 ha) shows >15 cm for extreme events in a confined reach, and small sites
    can *amplify* surges locally (Kiesel 2021).
  - Our boundary is forced by observed gauges, and SFINCS has no morphology. So we cannot see a
    bay-wide tidal response or the creek scour seen at Freiston (400 m yr⁻¹).
  - Suggested wording: "no measurable change in tidal high water outside the bank in this model".
    Also report the Sandy cells (up to 5 cm), and check low water and velocities in the outlet
    creek / Salem River.
- **Not covered, and the literature says it matters: groundwater.** Leaky gates have salted
  adjacent farm soil through groundwater (Kentuck Slough). The deep groundwater response is slow,
  over years (Veenklaas 2015). "Nothing changes outside" applies to surface water only. Seepage
  and salt under the banks are outside SFINCS, as §30 already notes.
- **Supported: subsided ground becomes open water, not marsh.** Diked marsh sits 0.3–1 m low.
  About 22% of breached Delaware Bay impoundments are still open water or mudflat, against 0.5% of
  never-diked marsh. Even PSEG's dredged sites kept elevation deficits. Middle Neck's p10 ground
  of −0.77 m fits this pattern. Wetland value per hectare in the economics should allow for years
  of open water.
- **Unknown: the Downe / PSEG claim.** It is a one-line municipal statement (2025) with no site,
  date or data. PSEG's salt-hay sites are in Commercial, Dennis and Maurice River townships. No
  primary source was found on whether PSEG protected adjacent uplands. **Do not cite it as
  evidence that restoration floods neighbours.**
- **Unknown:** no published or agency source describes Middle Neck itself, its bank, or its
  meadow company.

## Addendum (2026-10-06): the PSEG special issue, read in full

Source: *Ecological Engineering* vol. 25 (2005), all ten papers. The user downloaded them to
`docs/refs/`, which is gitignored (publisher PDFs; do not commit). Read as full text [PR][full].

- **PSEG did protect adjacent land, with inland dikes.** Teal & Weishar (2005, pp. 306-307,
  10.1016/j.ecoleng.2005.04.009):
  - Formal adaptive-management thresholds included "upland flooding beyond that which would
    have occurred without the restoration".
  - "Although a few triggers were observed, these were all related to some aspect of the
    upland dikes that were constructed to protect adjacent properties from flooding. While
    these were significant to local citizens and the regulators, they did not have a direct
    impact on the marsh restoration itself and are not discussed further."
  - Restoration management watched for "upland flooding, standing water, salt-water intrusion
    into local wells, and/or poor drainage of the marsh plain".
  - So: landward protection was built, it was needed (triggers were hit), and the papers
    give no details or numbers on it.
- **Neighbours' concerns were groundwater and septic, not only surface flooding.** Weishar,
  Teal & Hinkle (2005, pp. 234-236, 10.1016/j.ecoleng.2005.04.012):
  - The local public worried about "flood control, groundwater effects and potential damage
    to their wells or septic systems".
  - Sea-level rise had already raised groundwater near the sites, producing "brackish wells
    and flooded septic systems". EEP set up a shallow-aquifer testing and monitoring program
    "to separate impacts to septic systems that occurred from sea-level rise versus
    restoration".
  - Land was acquired in a "quiet phase" before going public, "to allow land acquisition
    without engendering land speculation".
- **Dikes give a false sense of security in storms** (Weishar et al. 2005, p. 233): "once the
  dike breeches, the marsh plain is catastrophically flooded, often resulting in flooding in
  the previously protected transitional and upland areas … storm waters are held on the marsh
  plain by the perimeter dikes, taking weeks or months to drain".
- **Subsidence and open water.**
  - Diked marsh plains lost "as much as 1 m" of relative elevation (Weishar et al. 2005,
    p. 236).
  - Hinkle & Mitsch (2005, 10.1016/j.ecoleng.2005.04.011): three sites, 1,780 ha, restored
    1996-98.
  - Restored ground evolved "from ponded water, to mudflat, to low marsh". At Commercial
    Township most of the site stayed inundated until 2000. By 2003 ponded water was down to
    8%, but non-vegetated marsh plain was still 47-50%.
  - Two sites met the vegetation goal 8 years ahead of the 12-year schedule.
- **Historical note** (Philipp 2005, 10.1016/j.ecoleng.2005.04.010): at Woodland Beach (DE), after
  an 1878 storm breach made open water, "landowners rebuilt the roadway dike" to keep it for
  waterfowl hunting. Roads on dikes are part of the historic meadow-bank system.

**For our Middle Neck result:** this is direct local support. The one large restoration of
diked land on this shore built upland dikes to protect adjacent properties, i.e. our set-back
design. It also monitored wells and septic systems for salt and groundwater effects, which
SFINCS cannot model. Caveat: these were salt-hay farms, not cropland.
